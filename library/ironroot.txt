Title: Ironroot
Hero: Kael Morrow, 14
Genre: Young Adult · Fantasy · Growth
Colors: #1f3b3a, #e8a33d
Blurb: The smallest, sickliest apprentice at the mountain forge can't even lift a hammer. Three hundred stone steps and one long winter later, the mountain will learn his name.

## Episode 1: The Smallest Apprentice

The cart left Kael Morrow at the bottom of Mount Harrow with a canvas bag, a letter of introduction, and a cough he'd had since he was six.

Above him, carved straight into the gray cliff, rose the steps. Three hundred of them, each one worn into a shallow bowl by centuries of boots. At the very top, smoke curled from the chimneys of Ironroot Forge, the most famous smithy in the northern kingdoms. The place where the king's swords were made. The place where boys went to become men who could bend iron.

Kael was fourteen. He weighed about as much as a sack of grain. His mother had written the letter herself and pressed it into his hands with tears in her eyes. Her brother had trained at Ironroot. It was the only favor the family had left to call in.

He started climbing.

By step fifty, his legs burned. By step one hundred, the cough came, deep and rattling, and he had to sit down until the world stopped spinning. By step two hundred, he was crawling. When he finally reached the top, it was nearly dark, and a group of apprentices stood in the forge yard watching him with open amusement.

"Look," said the tallest one, a broad-shouldered boy with a burn scar across his knuckles. "Somebody sent us a twig."

The others laughed. Kael pushed himself to his feet and held out the letter. "I'm here to see Master Brannoch."

The master was a mountain of a man with a gray beard braided into two thick ropes. He read the letter by the light of the furnace, his face unreadable. Then he looked Kael up and down, slowly, the way a smith looks at a bar of metal to find the flaws.

"Your uncle was a good smith," Brannoch said. "But you are not your uncle."

"No, sir."

Brannoch walked to the anvil, picked up a forging hammer, and held it out by the handle. "Lift this over your head."

Kael took it. The hammer was so heavy his arms dropped immediately. He set his feet, gritted his teeth, and heaved. It came up to his chest. Then his shoulders. His arms trembled violently. The head of the hammer wobbled, tipped, and crashed onto the stone floor, missing his foot by inches.

The tall boy, whose name Kael would soon learn was Dace, snorted loudly.

Brannoch picked the hammer back up as if it weighed nothing. "I'll give you one month," he said. "That's what I owe your uncle. After that, if you can't lift a hammer, you go home."

That night, Kael lay on a straw mattress in the apprentice hall, listening to the others snore. His arms ached. His chest rattled. Through the narrow window, he could see the stars over the mountain, cold and impossibly far away.

One month, he thought. He didn't know how it could possibly be enough. But he had climbed three hundred steps today, even if he'd crawled the last of them.

That was something. It would have to be.

## Episode 2: Water and Stone

Kael's first job at Ironroot Forge was not forging. It was water.

The quenching barrels needed to be full every morning, and the only well was at the bottom of the mountain. Three hundred steps down. Three hundred steps back up, with two wooden buckets on a yoke across his shoulders.

It was the job they gave to apprentices who weren't expected to last.

On the first morning, Kael made it forty steps up before the buckets pulled him backward and he fell, soaking himself and bruising his knee on the stone. He walked back down, refilled them, and tried again. He made it sixty steps. Then thirty. By noon, he had delivered one half-full bucket, and Dace poured it out onto the ground while the other apprentices watched, just to see his face.

That night, Kael couldn't lift his arms to eat. He sat at the end of the long table staring at his bowl of stew until someone sat down across from him.

It was Wren, the forge's cook. She was old, older than Brannoch, with a face like a dried apple and hands covered in faded burns. She pushed his bowl closer.

"You're climbing wrong," she said.

"I'm climbing as hard as I can."

"That's the problem." She tapped the table with one crooked finger. "You hit those steps like you're angry at them. You sprint, you gasp, you collapse. The mountain doesn't care how angry you are. The mountain is patient. You have to be more patient than the mountain."

"How?"

"Breathe in for two steps. Breathe out for two steps. Never faster. When the cough comes, stop, let it pass, and start again. Don't think about the top. Think about the next step. Only the next one."

The next morning, Kael tried it. In, two steps. Out, two steps. It felt ridiculously slow. Other apprentices passed him on their way down to the village, laughing. He kept the rhythm. When the cough came, he stopped and waited and started again.

He made it one hundred steps before he had to rest.

The next day, one hundred and ten.

He began counting. He named certain steps so he could tell how far he'd come. Step seventy-three had a crack shaped like a lightning bolt. Step one hundred and fifty had a tuft of purple moss growing from its edge. Step two hundred and twelve was where he always wanted to quit, so he called it Quitting Step, and every time he passed it, he said out loud, "Not today."

Two weeks went by. Then three. The bruises faded. The yoke wore a callus across his shoulders. One morning, he realized he hadn't coughed once on the whole climb.

On the last day of the month, Kael carried two full buckets from the well to the forge without stopping. He set them beside the quenching barrel and turned around to find Master Brannoch watching him from the doorway.

The master said nothing. He just walked to the anvil, picked up the forging hammer, and held it out.

Kael took it. He set his feet the way he set them on the steps. He breathed in. He breathed out.

He lifted the hammer over his head and held it there, shaking but steady, for a slow count of ten.

Brannoch nodded once. "Another month," he said, and walked away.

## Episode 3: The Hammer Knows

Lifting the hammer was one thing. Using it was another.

The other apprentices swung their hammers like they were fighting the metal. Dace was the strongest of them all. When he struck a glowing bar, sparks exploded across the forge and the anvil rang like a church bell. Everyone stopped to watch when Dace worked.

When Kael struck, the hammer barely bounced. The sound was a dull, embarrassing clink.

"Again," said Brannoch, standing over him. "Again. Again."

For weeks, Kael's blades came out crooked. Too thin in some places, too thick in others. Brannoch tossed them into the scrap bin without comment. Dace's blades went onto the rack for inspection, and the master praised them in front of everyone.

Then one evening, Brannoch stayed late and pulled one of Dace's swords off the rack. He handed it to Kael. "What's wrong with it?"

Kael turned it in the firelight. It looked perfect. Shining, heavy, strong. Then he ran his thumb gently along the flat of the blade, and felt it. Tiny ridges. Waves in the steel, where each massive hammer blow had landed slightly off.

"It's uneven," Kael said. "He hits hard, but he doesn't hit in the same place."

"Strength without control is just noise," Brannoch said. "Anyone can make noise. Your blows are weak, boy. But they're patient. They land where you put them. That's not nothing. That's the beginning of a smith."

After that, Kael stopped trying to swing like Dace. He swung like he climbed. Steady. Rhythmic. Breathe in, lift. Breathe out, strike. Each blow landing exactly where the last one had. His arms grew stronger every week from hauling water, and slowly, the dull clink became a clean, clear ring.

By midwinter, his blades stopped going into the scrap bin.

By late winter, Brannoch hung one of them on the rack, right next to Dace's, and the other apprentices crowded around to compare. Kael's was smaller, lighter, and so perfectly balanced it seemed to float in your hand.

Dace didn't say anything. But the next morning, when Kael went to the rack, his blade was on the floor, snapped in half. Someone had bent it across the anvil horn in the night.

Kael stood looking at the pieces for a long time. He knew who had done it. Everyone knew. The other apprentices watched him, waiting to see what he'd do. Some of them, he could tell, were hoping for a fight.

He thought about it. He thought about walking up to Dace, who was twice his size, and shoving him. He thought about breaking one of Dace's blades in return.

Then he picked up the two halves, carried them to the scrap bin, and dropped them in.

He walked to the furnace, pulled out a fresh bar of steel, and started again. Breathe in, lift. Breathe out, strike.

Behind him, the forge was very quiet. Dace stood by the bellows with his arms crossed, and for just a moment, he didn't look angry. He looked confused, like a boy who had swung his hardest at something, and missed.

## Episode 4: Winter Trial

Every year, at the end of winter, Ironroot held the Trial of the Pass.

Each apprentice had to carry a sack of iron ore from the old mine on the eastern ridge down to the forge. Six miles through snow and wind, along a narrow trail that clung to the side of the mountain. Those who finished would advance to journeyman training. Those who didn't would go home.

The morning of the trial, the sky was the color of a bruise. Wren pressed a cloth bundle of bread and dried meat into Kael's hands. "Remember," she said. "More patient than the mountain."

The sacks of ore weighed half as much as Kael did. Dace hoisted his onto one shoulder like it was full of feathers and set off at a jog, leaving the others behind. Kael settled his sack across his back, found his rhythm, and began to walk.

In, two steps. Out, two steps.

By the second hour, the snow started. By the third, it was so thick he could barely see the trail. One by one, other apprentices turned back, deciding to try again next year. Kael passed them, one after another, not because he was fast, but because he never stopped.

At the narrowest part of the pass, where the trail was barely wider than his boots and the cliff dropped away into white nothing, he heard someone shouting.

It was Dace. He was lying on a ledge a few feet below the trail, his ore sack split open, his left leg bent at an angle that made Kael's stomach turn. He'd been going too fast. He'd slipped on the ice.

"Go," Dace said through clenched teeth when Kael climbed down to him. "Just go. You'll fail the trial if you stop."

Kael looked at the trail. He looked at the snow, falling harder now. He looked at Dace, the boy who had poured out his water and broken his blade and called him a twig on his very first night.

Then he set down his own ore sack, crouched, and pulled Dace's arm across his shoulders.

"You can't carry me," Dace said. "You're half my size."

"I carried water up three hundred steps every day for four months," Kael said. "You're just a bigger bucket."

It took him four hours. Dace leaned on him, hopping on one leg, and when he couldn't hop anymore, Kael carried him on his back. In, two steps. Out, two steps. When the cough came back in the cold, he stopped and let it pass and started again. He didn't think about the forge. He thought about the next step. Only the next one.

They staggered into the forge yard after dark. Master Brannoch was waiting by the gate with a lantern. He took one look at Dace's leg and shouted for Wren.

Then he turned to Kael. "Where's your ore?"

"On the mountain," Kael said. "I left it."

"Then you failed the trial."

"Yes, sir."

Brannoch looked at him for a very long time, the lantern hissing in the wind. Then he said something Kael didn't expect. "Go eat. Get warm. We'll talk in the morning."

That night, from the infirmary, Dace called his name. Kael went to the doorway.

"Why?" Dace asked quietly. "After everything."

Kael thought about it. "Because the mountain doesn't care who's strong," he said. "It just cares who keeps going."

## Episode 5: Ironroot

The thaw came early that spring, and with it, disaster.

The village at the foot of Mount Harrow sat on the other side of the Greywater River. For a hundred years, the only crossing was an old chain bridge, its links forged at Ironroot by masters long dead. Kael crossed it every week to buy flour for Wren.

On the third night of the thaw, the snowmelt turned the Greywater into a roaring brown monster. In the morning, a boy came running up the three hundred steps, white with panic. One of the great chains had snapped. The bridge was hanging by a single cable, and half the village children were trapped on the far bank, where they'd gone to school the day before.

Brannoch was already pulling on his leather apron. "The new link has to be forged here and carried down. Fast. Before the second chain goes."

The apprentices froze. A bridge link was massive, as thick as a man's wrist. Forging one would take the strongest arms in the forge, and the strongest arms in the forge belonged to Dace, who was still on crutches.

"Kael," said Brannoch. "You're on the anvil."

Kael stared at him. "Me?"

"A bridge link isn't a sword. It doesn't need to be pretty. It needs every blow in the exact same place, for hours, without a single weak spot. I know only one apprentice who strikes like that."

So Kael forged. Brannoch worked the bellows. Dace, leaning on his crutches, called out the heat of the metal by its color, his eye as sharp as ever. Breathe in, lift. Breathe out, strike. The hammer rang clean and clear, again and again, hundreds of times, until Kael's arms were on fire and the sweat ran into his eyes. He didn't stop. He was more patient than the iron.

When it was done, the link was ugly and black and perfect.

Kael carried it down all three hundred steps himself. He passed the lightning bolt crack, and the purple moss, and Quitting Step, where he said out loud, without breaking stride, "Not today."

At the river, he and Brannoch hammered the new link into place while the water thundered beneath them. When the chain pulled tight and the bridge lifted level, a cheer went up from both banks. One by one, the children ran across to their families.

That night, there was a feast in the forge hall. Brannoch stood at the head of the long table, and the room went quiet.

"Kael Morrow failed the Trial of the Pass," he said. "He left his ore on the mountain to carry another apprentice home. In two hundred years of trials, that has happened exactly once before." He paused. "The other apprentice was me. And the one who carried me was his uncle."

Kael's breath caught.

"The trial was never about the ore," Brannoch said. "It was about whether you would keep going. Kael Morrow, you are a journeyman of Ironroot."

The hall erupted. Dace pounded the table with his crutch. Wren wiped her eyes with her apron.

Later, when everyone was asleep, Kael walked out to the top of the steps and looked down at the long stone staircase, silver in the moonlight. Three hundred steps. On his first night, they had looked impossible.

He understood now. You don't become strong all at once. You become strong one step at a time. And then one day you look back, and the mountain is beneath you.
