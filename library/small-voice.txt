Title: Small Voice
Hero: Nora Vance, 15
Genre: Young Adult · Growth
Colors: #3b2a6b, #ff5a6e
Blurb: Nora freezes every time she has to speak. Then the school's dying radio station needs someone to fill the silence, and the only one in the booth is her.

## Episode 1: The Silent Seat

Nora Vance had a rule. Sit in the back. Sit by the window. Never raise your hand.

The rule had worked for three years. It stopped working on a Tuesday in October, when Mr. Halloran tapped his clipboard and said, "Nora. You're up."

The presentation was five minutes on the history of radio. She had practiced it eleven times in her bedroom. She knew every word. But when she reached the front of the room and turned around, twenty-eight faces looked back at her, and every one of those words went somewhere she couldn't follow.

"Radio," she said. Then nothing.

Her note cards shook. Her throat closed like a fist. Somewhere near the front, Kendra Pike leaned toward her friend and whispered, loud enough to carry, "Is she going to cry?"

Nora did not cry. Not in the classroom. She said, "Sorry," and walked back to her seat by the window, and Mr. Halloran said, "We'll try again next week," in a voice that meant he wouldn't make her.

She cried on the bus instead, with her hood up and her forehead against the cold glass.

At home, the house smelled like cinnamon and old paper. Her grandmother, June, had moved in over the summer, along with forty-one cardboard boxes that still lined the hallway. Nora dropped her bag and kicked the nearest box harder than she meant to. It tipped. Something heavy slid out and thumped onto the carpet.

It was a microphone. Silver, scratched, shaped like a small rocket with a grille across its face. Beneath it was a stack of cassette tapes, each labeled in neat blue ink. June after Midnight. Show 112. Show 113. Show 240.

"Careful with that," said a voice behind her. Grandma June stood in the kitchen doorway, drying a mug. "That microphone has been dropped by better people than you."

"You were on the radio?"

"Nineteen years. Overnight shift. Midnight to five." June smiled. "Truckers, nurses, insomniacs, lonely people. The ones who are awake when everyone else is asleep."

Nora turned the microphone over in her hands. "Weren't you scared? Talking to all those people?"

June laughed, a big laugh, the kind Nora had never been able to make. "Sweetheart, the first night I went on air, I said good evening and then didn't say another word for forty seconds. Do you know how long forty seconds of silence is on the radio? It's a lifetime."

"What happened?"

"A trucker called in and said, 'Lady, are you okay?' And I said, 'No.' And then I told him why." June set down the mug. "Turns out that's all anybody wants. Somebody honest on the other end."

That night, Nora lay awake with the microphone on her nightstand. It wasn't plugged into anything. It didn't need to be. She stared at it until the streetlight outside went pale with dawn, thinking about forty seconds, and a voice that eventually came back.

## Episode 2: Static

The radio station at Kessler High was called WKVR, and almost nobody knew it existed.

It lived in a converted supply closet at the end of the science wing, behind a door with a hand-lettered sign that said On Air, Please Knock. Nora found it on a Thursday, after walking past it twice to make sure no one was watching.

Inside, a senior named Theo Okafor sat surrounded by milk crates full of vinyl records, a soldering iron balanced on a coffee mug. He looked up. "You lost?"

"I saw the flyer," Nora said. Her voice came out small and crooked. "Volunteers."

"Oh. Wow. Nobody's ever answered the flyer." Theo stood up so fast he knocked over a crate. "Okay. What do you want to do? We need hosts. We need everything, honestly. Last semester we had four people. Now it's me and a guy who only shows up to play metal on Fridays."

"I could sort the records," Nora said quickly. "Organize things. Behind the scenes."

Theo looked at her for a moment, and she braced herself for disappointment. Instead, he just nodded. "Behind the scenes is important. Records are by genre, then alphabetical. Good luck. It's a war zone."

So Nora sorted. Every day after school she came to the closet, pulled records from the crates, and slid them into place. Jazz. Folk. Soul. A Spanish guitar album with a cracked sleeve. Theo talked while he worked, and she listened, and slowly she started to say things back. Small things. Which album covers she liked. What her grandmother used to play.

One evening, Theo left early for a dentist appointment and tossed her the key. "Lock up when you're done."

The closet was quiet. The booth, a little glass box in the corner with a microphone on a swinging arm, was empty. The red On Air light was off.

Nora sat down in the chair. Just to see how it felt.

She found the record button on the soundboard. She pressed it. She leaned toward the microphone and said, "Hello. This is Nora."

Then she played it back.

Her voice sounded like a stranger's. Thin. Wobbly. Every word seemed to apologize for itself. She hit delete so fast her finger slipped.

At home, she told Grandma June about it while they washed dishes. "I sound terrible. Like a mouse that's about to get stepped on."

June handed her a plate to dry. "Everybody hates their recorded voice. That's not a flaw, that's biology. Your skull makes your voice sound deeper inside your own head."

"It's not just the sound. It's how I say things. Like I'm sorry for being there."

June turned off the water. She was quiet for a moment, which was unusual for her.

"Nora," she said. "Your voice isn't the problem. Your silence is. You've been practicing silence for years, and you've gotten very, very good at it. Now you have to practice the other thing. Badly, at first. That's the only way anybody gets good at anything."

The next evening, Nora stayed late again. She recorded herself reading the back of a record sleeve. She hated it. She did it again. And again.

By Friday, she had recorded twenty-two takes. She didn't delete the last one.

## Episode 3: Dead Air

It happened on a Wednesday in November, the way bad things always do, without warning.

WKVR broadcast for one hour every afternoon, from three to four, to a listening audience Theo optimistically estimated at "maybe forty." The show was supposed to be Theo, playing records and reading school announcements. But at two fifty-one, Nora's phone buzzed.

It was a text from Theo. Food poisoning. Cafeteria tacos. Can't stand up. Can you just let the playlist run? It's queued.

Nora looked at the soundboard. The playlist was queued. That was fine. She could push a button. Anyone could push a button.

At three o'clock, she pushed it. The first song started, a slow soul track with warm horns. She breathed out.

At three twenty-two, the playlist ended.

Theo had queued twenty-two minutes of music for a sixty-minute show. He must have meant to talk between songs. Announcements. Weather. Jokes. The things a host does.

The music stopped, and nothing replaced it.

Dead air. On the soundboard, the level meters dropped flat. Somewhere out there, maybe forty people were listening to nothing.

Nora's hands went cold. Her heart pounded so hard she could feel it in her teeth. Five seconds. Ten seconds. She thought about running. She thought about pulling the plug and blaming a technical problem.

Fifteen seconds. Twenty.

Then she thought about her grandmother, alone in a booth decades ago, saying good evening and then nothing. Forty seconds. And a trucker on the other end of the line asking if she was okay.

Nora reached out and flipped the microphone switch. The red light came on.

"Hi," she said. Her voice cracked right down the middle. "Um. Sorry. This is WKVR. Theo is sick. I'm Nora. I'm not really a host. I sort the records."

She stopped. The silence came back, enormous. She made herself keep going.

"I don't know what to say, so I'm going to read you something. My grandmother used to do overnight radio, and she said the people listening at strange hours are usually the lonely ones. So if that's you, this is for you."

She pulled a poetry book from the shelf, the first one her hand found, and read a poem about a lighthouse keeper. Her voice shook the whole way through. She stumbled twice. When she finished, she put on a record, any record, and turned off the microphone and put her head down on the desk and shook.

Then the station phone lit up.

She stared at it. Nobody ever called the station phone. Hesitantly, she picked up.

"Hey," said a voice. A boy, she thought, maybe a freshman. "Was that you reading? The lighthouse thing?"

"Yes. Sorry."

"Don't be sorry. That was really nice. I'm in the nurse's office. Bad day. It helped." A pause. "Are you going to do it again?"

Nora looked at the microphone. Her hands were still shaking. But under the fear, there was something else, small and warm, like a pilot light catching.

"Maybe," she said. "Yeah. Maybe."

When she got home, she found three texts from numbers she didn't know, all from people who'd been listening. One just said, Keep going.

## Episode 4: The Late Show

Theo recovered from the tacos, heard the recording, and immediately gave Nora a show.

"Thursdays," he said. "Thirty minutes. Do whatever you want."

"I don't know what I want."

"Then figure it out on air. That's what everybody does."

She called it Small Voice. The idea came to her at two in the morning, the way her grandmother said good ideas usually do. Every week, she would interview one person at Kessler High who nobody ever heard from. The quiet ones. The ones in the back row by the window.

Her first guest was Mr. Delgado, the night custodian, who had worked at the school for twenty-six years and knew every secret the building held. He told stories about the ghost in the band room, which turned out to be a raccoon, and about the year the whole senior class tried to fit a cow in the elevator. Nora barely had to say anything. She just asked, and listened, and asked again.

Her second guest was a sophomore named Priya who wrote songs on a ukulele but had never played one for anyone. She played one live, her voice trembling just like Nora's had, and when it was done, the station phone rang four times.

By the fifth week, people were stopping Nora in the hallway. Not many. A few. A girl from chemistry class said, "I liked the one with the custodian." A boy she'd never met gave her a thumbs up by the vending machines. Each time, Nora's face went hot, and each time, she made herself look up and say thank you.

Then came the message.

It arrived through the station's anonymous request form, between a request for a pop song and a request for more raccoon stories. It said: Who cares what the weird quiet girl thinks. Nobody listens to this. Get off the air.

Nora knew who'd sent it before she even checked the timestamp. Third period. The time Kendra Pike always sat on her phone in the back of study hall.

She showed it to Theo. He read it and frowned. "You want me to block the form?"

"No," Nora said. She was surprised by how calm she sounded. "Leave it."

She showed it to Grandma June that night. June read it twice and handed the phone back.

"In nineteen years of radio," June said, "I got hate mail every single week. You know what it taught me? You don't make that kind of noise unless you're listening."

That Thursday, Nora did something she had never planned to do. At the end of her show, she read the message out loud, word for word, on the air.

"Somebody sent this in," she said. "And I just want to say: I hear you. A year ago, I would've agreed with you. I would've gotten off the air. But I think the point of this show is that quiet people have something worth hearing. Including, maybe, whoever wrote this. So if you ever want to be a guest, the door's open."

She turned off the microphone. Her hands were steady. She noticed that only afterward, walking home in the early dark, and the noticing made her smile the whole way.

## Episode 5: Loud Enough

The email came in March. Budget cuts. The school board was voting to close WKVR at the end of the year and turn the closet back into storage.

Theo slammed his laptop shut. "That's it. That's the end. They don't care about forty listeners."

"It's two hundred and twelve listeners," Nora said. "I checked the stream numbers."

He looked at her. "Since when do you check the stream numbers?"

"Since it started mattering."

There was a public comment period at the board meeting. Anyone could sign up to speak for three minutes. Theo couldn't go. He had a college visit that night, booked months ago, and he spent ten minutes apologizing about it. That left Nora.

Three minutes. In the school auditorium. In front of the board, the principal, the parents, the reporters from the local paper, and anyone else who showed up.

She wrote her speech eleven times. It was the same number of times she had practiced her history presentation in October, which felt like a joke the universe was playing on her.

The night of the meeting, the auditorium was almost full. Nora sat in the third row, which was the closest she had ever voluntarily sat to the front of anything. Grandma June sat beside her, holding the old silver microphone in her lap like a good luck charm.

"You don't need it," June whispered. "But it wanted to come."

When they called her name, Nora walked to the podium. She turned around.

Hundreds of faces. More than in the classroom. Bright lights. And for one terrible second, the words went away again, just like before. Her note cards trembled. Her throat started to close.

She let the silence sit there. Five seconds. Ten. She didn't run.

Then she put the note cards face down on the podium.

"My name is Nora Vance," she said. "Six months ago, I couldn't give a five-minute presentation without freezing. A lot of you probably still remember that, because it was pretty bad."

A few people laughed, gently. She kept going.

"WKVR is where I learned to talk. Not because anyone made me. Because somebody gave me a microphone and a room where it was okay to be bad at it first. Every week, I interview someone at this school who doesn't usually get heard. Two hundred and twelve people listen. Some of them have told me it's the only part of their week where they feel like someone like them matters." She took a breath. "You can turn that room back into a closet. But I think you'd be closing a door that some kids really need to find."

She didn't use all three minutes. She didn't need to.

The board voted to keep WKVR for one more year, on a trial basis. It wasn't forever. It was enough.

The next Thursday, a new message came through the request form. It wasn't anonymous this time. It said: Can I be a guest? I've never told anyone this, but I get really scared too. Kendra.

Nora read it three times. Then she turned on the microphone, watched the red light glow, and said, in a voice that was clear, and steady, and entirely her own, "Good afternoon. This is Small Voice. And today, we have a special guest."
